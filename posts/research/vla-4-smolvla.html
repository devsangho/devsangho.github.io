If you understand π0, SmolVLA is much easier, because the principle is the same: a VLM and an action expert, with flow matching producing an action chunk. What differs is the constraint. The whole model has 450 million parameters and has to run on a single consumer GPU.

That does not make it π0 with the VLM swapped for a smaller one. The VLM, the action expert, and the way the two are connected are all different, and the execution system that runs after training was designed separately.

1Architecture

One design decision matters here. The VLM is frozen and only the action expert is trained. This differs from π0, which trains the VLM as well, and it is worth comparing with the OpenVLA experiment in Part 1, where freezing the vision encoder hurt performance badly. Of the roughly 450 million total parameters, about 100 million are actually trained.

The flow matching principle is the same as in Part 3. The time τ is sampled from a Beta distribution, following π0, and inference is fixed at 10 steps. In the loss ablation, the LIBERO average is 80.25 for flow matching and 75.25 for L1 regression, so flow matching did better in this setup.

NoteThe paper writes both \(A^\tau=\tau A+(1-\tau)\epsilon\) and the target \(u=\epsilon-A\). Differentiating that expression gives \(dA^\tau/d\tau=A-\epsilon\), which has the opposite sign from the target. The paper alone does not settle which convention was used, so if you need to work with an implementation, it is best to check the time direction in the code.

2How It Got Small

Parameter count and compute are separate matters. Fewer parameters means less weight memory, and fewer tokens means less attention computation. SmolVLA works on both.

What was reducedHow
VLM sizeSmolVLM-2 instead of PaliGemma at about 3B
Visual tokensNo tiling, one global image + pixel shuffle, 64 tokens per frame
VLM layersOnly the first 16 layers, half of the total (\(N=L/2\))
Expert width0.75 times the VLM hidden size \(d\)

When the hidden dimension is halved, the size of the attention weight matrices scales roughly with the square and drops to about 1/4. That does not mean the whole model shrinks by the same amount, but it does explain why reducing width is effective.

VLM or Action Expert: Where to Spend the Budget

Shrinking each one costs something different. A smaller VLM may miss relations such as "pick up the red cup behind the blue cup," which can weaken its understanding of what to do. A smaller action expert may know where the cup is but be less able to express how to move, such as wrist pose and gripper timing.

The LIBERO ablations in the paper give a few hints on this question.

These are simulation results from a small sample, and it is hard to draw a general conclusion for either side, but "do we really need a large VLM?" remains a question that has to be answered by experiment. It would be useful to compare different splits of the same total parameter budget between the VLM and the expert.

3The Connection: Cross-Attention

In π0 from Part 3, the VLM tokens and the action expert tokens met in the self-attention of a single transformer. SmolVLA is different. The action expert produces the Query, and the two are connected through cross-attention that uses the VLM features as Key and Value. The VLM strictly becomes the side that provides conditioning, which helps with reuse and compute optimization.

Inside the action expert, each block uses either cross-attention (CA) or self-attention (SA). SA uses a causal mask, so each action token sees only the earlier tokens in the chunk. This is what distinguishes it from π0's bidirectional attention. The paper explains that this SA makes chunks smoother.

4Asynchronous Inference

If the next chunk is computed only after the whole current chunk has been executed, the robot stops until inference finishes. SmolVLA separates execution from inference. The RobotClient pulls actions from a queue and executes them, while a PolicyServer, which may be remote, computes new chunks.

When the fraction left in the queue falls below a threshold \(g\), a new observation is sent.

$$\frac{|\mathcal A_t|}{n}For a new chunk to arrive before the queue runs out, the following is required. \(\Delta t\) is the control period, which is 33 ms at 30 fps.

$$g\ge\frac{\mathbb E[\ell_S]/\Delta t}{n}$$

With \(g=0\) execution is fully sequential, \(g=0.7\) is the paper's asynchronous example, and with \(g=1\) an observation is sent at every timestep. Similar observations are filtered out by joint-space distance, and a new chunk is merged with the overlapping part of the existing chunk.

Asynchronous execution is not a learning algorithm. It is a system design for running a trained policy. It also has a cost. The robot keeps moving while the next chunk is computed, so the observation used for inference becomes stale, and chunks can conflict with each other.

5Community Data

SmolVLA's pretraining is based on SO-100 data published on Hugging Face: 481 datasets, 22.9K episodes, and 10.6 million frames. Unlike lab data, the labels are messy, so preprocessing was necessary.

  • Task descriptions: Useless labels such as "Hold", "Up", and "task desc" were rewritten with Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct, which was shown sampled frames.
  • Camera names: Names that differed across datasets were standardized by mapping them by hand to top, wrist, and side.

The effect was large. The SO-100 average success rate rose from 40% for single-task training without pretraining, to 51.7% for multi-task training without pretraining, to 78.3% with community pretraining. Pretraining ran for 200,000 steps with AdamW and a cosine learning rate, and used 4 GPUs for large batches.

This does not mean a small model is always better than a large one. The paper states these limitations.

  • Pretraining is limited to SO-100 data, so generalization to other robots has not been verified.
  • There are about 23,000 trajectories, far fewer than OpenVLA's roughly 1 million.
  • The VLM used was pretrained mainly on documents and OCR.
  • Tasks that need long-horizon planning are still weak.

The terms from these four posts fall into three categories.

  • Techniques for understanding information: VLM, transformer, attention, attention mask.
  • Techniques for generating and learning actions: action expert, regression, flow matching, action chunk, loss.
  • Techniques for running fast: caching, asynchronous inference, replanning.

When reading a new VLA paper, start by finding which of these it changed. After working through the four posts, three questions remain.

  1. How much does the way VLM information is passed to the action expert, that is, the coupling strength, affect performance and cost?
  2. How often are multimodal actions needed? In other words, where is the boundary between direct regression and flow matching?
  3. How should the same parameter budget be divided between visual and language understanding and action generation?

None of these is a matter of comparing how intelligent the models are. They are questions of how to connect networks and where to spend computation when given the same information.
